1 Why we measure with universal units 1.1.2 S1

A measurement is a comparison with a standard: how many of that standard fit the quantity you are describing. Without an agreed standard, “about two sticks long” cannot be checked by anyone else.

Scientists use the Système International (SI) A shared metric system of units so a measurement in one country can be checked in another. so that a length, a mass or a time recorded in Madrid can be repeated in Helsinki. That is what universal units are for: results can be compared, reproduced and criticised.

  • Write the number and the unit every time (4.7 cm, not “4.7”).
  • Choose a unit that keeps the number a sensible size (2.3 km, not 2 300 000 mm).
  • The same investigation, done again, should be able to get a similar result.

2 SI base units

Seven base units sit at the root of SI. S1 laboratory work uses the first five constantly. The last two appear later in the course.

QuantityUnitSymbol
lengthmetre\(m\)
masskilogram\(kg\)
timesecond\(s\)
electric currentampere\(A\)
temperaturekelvin\(K\)
amount of substancemole\(\mathrm{mol}\)
luminous intensitycandela\(\mathrm{cd}\)

The litre (\(1\,\mathrm{L} = 1\,\mathrm{dm}^3 = 1000\,\mathrm{cm}^3\)) is a handy volume unit in the lab, but it is not a base unit. The newton is a derived unit of force.

Mass is the kilogram, not the gram. Temperature in SI is the kelvin. Celsius uses the same degree size but starts at a different zero (\(0\,\mathrm{^\circ C} = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}\)).

3 SI prefixes

A prefix scales a unit by a power of ten. A letter in front of a unit symbol that multiplies it by a power of ten (kilo = 1000, milli = 0.001). Write the prefix letter immediately in front of the unit symbol: km, mg, µs.

PrefixSymbolFactorMeaning
gigaG\(10^9\)1 000 000 000
megaM\(10^6\)1 000 000
kilok\(10^3\)1000
centic\(10^{-2}\)0.01
millim\(10^{-3}\)0.001
microµ\(10^{-6}\)0.000001
nanon\(10^{-9}\)0.000000001

Going down the table (to a smaller unit) makes the number larger: \(2\,\mathrm{m} = 2000\,\mathrm{mm}\). Going up makes the number smaller: \(3000\,\mathrm{mm} = 3\,\mathrm{m}\).

4 Converting units

Conversion is multiplying or dividing by the right power of ten. Keep the quantity the same; only the unit label changes.

  1. Write the number with its current unit.
  2. Multiply (smaller unit) or divide (larger unit) by the factor.
  3. Check that the new size is sensible: a classroom is about 8 m, not 8 km.

Worked example: \(2.4\,\mathrm{km}\) to metres. Kilo means 1000, so \(2.4 \times 1000 = 2400\,\mathrm{m}\). Check: a few kilometres should become a few thousand metres, not 24 m.

Worked example: \(3.2\,\mathrm{cm}\) to metres. Centi means \(10^{-2}\), so divide by 100: \(0.032\,\mathrm{m}\).

5 Instruments, scales and calibration

Choose an instrument that can resolve the quantity you care about: a 30 cm ruler for a pencil, a measuring cylinder for a liquid, a digital balance for mass, a stopwatch for time.

On an analogue scale, read from directly in front so your eye is perpendicular to the mark (avoids parallax). Estimate to the nearest half or tenth of a division when the pointer sits between marks.

Calibration Checking an instrument against a known standard and adjusting it, or recording the correction. A 100 g standard mass or a reference thermometer is a typical check. A balance that reads 2 g when empty has a zero error — that is a calibration problem, not bad luck.

Worked example: A group places a 100.0 g standard on a balance and reads 102.3 g. The instrument is 2.3 g high. Subtract 2.3 g from later readings, or recalibrate, rather than averaging more wrong masses.

6 Accuracy, precision and error

Accuracy How close a measurement is to the true value. Precision How tightly repeats cluster, even if that cluster is in the wrong place.

  • Random error scatters repeats (reaction time, parallax). Reduce it by repeating and taking the mean.
  • Systematic error shifts every reading the same way (zero error, a damaged ruler). Calibration or a better instrument is the fix; averaging will not remove it.
  • The range of a set is largest minus smallest. The mean is the sum divided by how many readings you took.

Worked example: Three timings of the same fall are \(1.52\,\mathrm{s}\), \(1.48\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(1.50\,\mathrm{s}\). Mean \(= (1.52 + 1.48 + 1.50)/3 = 1.50\,\mathrm{s}\). Range \(= 1.52 - 1.48 = 0.04\,\mathrm{s}\). The cluster is tight (precise). If a stopwatch always starts 0.20 s late, the mean stays 0.20 s high — that is systematic error.

Quick reference

SI base units

  • length \(m\) · mass \(kg\) · time \(s\)
  • current \(A\) · temperature \(K\)
  • litre is useful, not a base unit

Prefixes

  • kilo \(10^3\) · mega \(10^6\) · giga \(10^9\)
  • centi \(10^{-2}\) · milli \(10^{-3}\) · micro \(10^{-6}\)

Quality of data

  • Accurate: close to the true value
  • Precise: repeats cluster together
  • Calibrate against a known standard
  • Range = max − min · mean = sum / n
