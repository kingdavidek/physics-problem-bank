What Is Science?

S1 Integrated Science · Unit 1.1 Science Lab · syllabus 1.1.1. Science is a public way of testing ideas: evidence, reproducibility, peer critique, and explanations that can improve.

1 Reliable knowledge, not just opinion 1.1.1 S1

An opinion can be honest and still be wrong. Scientific knowledge is more reliable when it is tied to evidence — observations and measurements that another person could, in principle, check.

Authority is not enough. A famous presenter, a confident classmate or a neat textbook diagram can still be mistaken. The question is always: how was this tested?

2 Evidence that can be tested

Evidence is not a rumour and not a slogan. It is a record of what was done and what was observed: a table, a method, a graph, a photograph of a scale.

A hypothesis is a testable idea about what might happen and why. If you cannot imagine a fair test that would challenge it, it is not yet a scientific hypothesis.

3 Reproducibility

A result is reproducible Another group can follow the same method and get a similar outcome. One surprising trial is a clue, not a law. Repeats also show scatter. Small differences are normal in measurement. Large unexplained gaps mean the method, the instrument or the recording needs another look.

Worked example: Two groups melt the same ice sample with the same method and both record about \(0\,\mathrm{^\circ C}\). That is reproducibility. A third group that skipped stirring and got \(8\,\mathrm{^\circ C}\) has not disproved the others — the method was not the same.

This lesson has no extra checkpoint here: the idea is practised in the quiz and in the classroom investigation.

4 Peer critique

Peer critique is not insulting the person. It is checking the method and the evidence: Was a control missing? Was the scale read from an angle? Was a result dropped because it looked inconvenient? Checking the method and the evidence — not insulting the person who did the work.

Sharing the method is part of being scientific. A secret “miracle test” cannot be reproduced, so it cannot be criticised fairly.

5 Provisional explanations

Scientific explanations are provisional: they are the best current account, not a promise that nothing will ever improve them. A scientific explanation that can change if better evidence appears. New measurements, a wider range of conditions, or a better model can replace an old story.

That is not the same as “anything goes”. A failed fair test is a reason to revise the model, not to invent data or to appeal to fame.

Make it scientific
