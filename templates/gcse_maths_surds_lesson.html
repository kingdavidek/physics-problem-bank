Surds

A surd is an exact square root that cannot be written as a whole number or simple fraction. Higher-tier GCSE questions often insist on surd form rather than decimals.

1 What Is a Surd? Higher

Example: √4 = 2 and √9 = 3 are not surds — they simplify to integers. √2, √3 and √5 are surds because they stay in root form for an exact answer.

When a question says “leave your answer in surd form” or “exact value”, do not round to a decimal.

2 The Two Key Rules

√a × √b = √(ab)   ·   √a ÷ √b = √(a/b)   ·   √a × √a = a

Example: √3 × √5 = √15. Also √20 ÷ √5 = √4 = 2.

Simplify √2 × √8.

3 Simplifying Surds

Example: √48 = √(16 × 3) = √16 × √3 = 4√3.

Find the largest square factor under the root, then split using √a × √b = √(ab).

Tip: Using a smaller square factor (e.g. √48 = 2√12) means you must simplify again. Always use the largest square factor first.

Write √72 in simplest surd form.

4 Adding and Subtracting Surds

Only combine surds with the same number under the root — like collecting like terms.

Example: √3 + √12 = √3 + 2√3 = 3√3 (simplify √12 first).

Simplify √8 + √2.

5 Expanding Brackets

Example: (2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 3 = 1 (difference of two squares).

(a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b   ·   (a + √b)² = a² + 2a√b + b

Example: (3 + √5)² = 9 + 6√5 + 5 = 14 + 6√5.

Expand (5 − √2)(5 + √2).

6 Rationalising the Denominator

Simple: 6/√3 = (6√3)/(√3×√3) = 6√3/3 = 2√3 — multiply top and bottom by √3.

Compound: Multiply by the conjugate (change the sign in the middle).

Conjugate of (a + √b) is (a − √b)  ·  (a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b

Example: 2/(1 + √5) → multiply by (1 − √5) → simplify to (√5 − 1)/2.
