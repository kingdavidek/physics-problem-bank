1 Brief and learning objectives IBL S2

Investigation: How does the angle of incidence affect the angle of reflection at a plane mirror?

This joins the Universe light lesson with Solar System geometry (straight rays, shadows, the idea that alignment matters). You need a ray you can see, a way to measure two angles from the normal, and repeats.

By the end of the practical you should be able to:

  • write a testable question and name independent, dependent and control variables;
  • measure angle of incidence and angle of reflection with a protractor from a drawn normal;
  • record repeats with units in the table headings;
  • say whether i = r within the limits of the method, and what you would change next.

If your teacher sets a lens or pinhole version instead, keep the same planning rules: one independent variable, SI-friendly units, and no looking at the Sun through any instrument.

2 Planning and variables
  • Independent: angle of incidence (for example 20°, 40°, 60° — values your teacher approves).
  • Dependent: angle of reflection, in degrees.
  • Controls: same plane mirror, same ray box or lamp, same distance from the slit to the mirror, same room lighting, same way of drawing the normal.

Change one independent variable at a time. Write a method another group could follow: how you mark the ray, how you draw the normal, where you place the protractor, and how many repeats.

Draft your question in your lab book: “How does _____ affect _____ when _____ is kept the same?”

3 Safety and risk
  • Ray boxes and lamps get hot. Allow cooling before packing away. Do not touch the bulb housing.
  • Do not look into the lamp, a laser, or a focused beam. Lasers only if the teacher supplies them and has assessed the risk.
  • Never look at the Sun through a telescope, binoculars, a lens or a mirror arrangement.
  • Tie back long hair. Bags off the floor. No running with glass or metal stands.
  • Your teacher’s risk assessment is the one that counts. Add any extra hazard they name.
4 Data table and graph

Put units in the headings. Keep every raw reading, then calculate the mean. Do not delete an inconvenient value without saying so in the evaluation.

A simple graph: independent variable on the horizontal axis (angle of incidence), mean reflection on the vertical axis. Label both axes with quantity and unit. If i = r, the points should lie close to a straight line through the origin with gradient 1, within protractor error.

5 Analysis and evaluation

Look at the pattern: as the angle of incidence increases, does the mean angle of reflection rise with it? Quote numbers from the table, not only a feeling.

Was the result reproducible? Compare repeats at the same incidence. A large scatter means a thick ray, a poorly drawn normal, or a protractor that slipped.

Evaluation prompts:

  • What limited the accuracy (ray width, protractor resolution, the mirror not being plane, a normal that was not perpendicular)?
  • Would a mean of more repeats reduce random scatter?
  • Would a systematic offset (always reading 2° high) need a better method rather than averaging?
  • What would you change in the next trial so another group could trust the evidence?
