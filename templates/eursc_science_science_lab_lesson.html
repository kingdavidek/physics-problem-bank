1 Choosing instruments 1.1.3 S1

Match the instrument to the quantity: a balance for mass, a measuring cylinder for liquid volume (read the bottom of the meniscus), a ruler or tape for length, a thermometer for temperature, a stopwatch for time.

The instrument must also resolve the change you care about. A thermometer marked every 1 °C is a poor choice if you expect a 0.2 °C difference.

2 Safety
  • Wear eye protection when heating or using chemicals.
  • Tie back long hair; no running; bags off the floor.
  • Never taste laboratory chemicals. Smell only if your teacher shows a safe waft.
  • Point heating tubes away from people. Wipe spills so nobody slips.

4 Variables
  • Independent — the factor you change on purpose.
  • Dependent — the outcome you measure.
  • Control — everything else you keep the same so the test is fair.

Example: How does water temperature affect dissolving time of sugar? Independent = temperature; dependent = time; controls = mass of sugar, volume of water, stirring.

6 Reducing error

Random scatter (reaction time, parallax) is reduced by repeats and a mean, and by reading scales from in front. A systematic offset (a stopwatch that always reads 0.4 s high) needs calibration or a correction; averaging will not remove it.

8 Evaluating the method
