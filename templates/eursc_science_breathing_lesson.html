1 Gases in air 1.3.3 S1

Ordinary air is a mixture of gases. It is mostly nitrogen (\(N_2\)), with a smaller share of oxygen (\(O_2\)), plus a little carbon dioxide (\(CO_2\)) and other gases. It is not mostly helium and it is not “thoughts”.

GasShare of dry air (approx.)
nitrogen78%
oxygen21%
carbon dioxide0.04%

Those percentages are a classroom model of dry air. Water vapour varies with weather and is left out of this table.

2 Inhaled and exhaled air

Exhaled air usually has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than inhaled air. Nitrogen stays about the same: it is not used up in respiration.

GasInhaled (approx.)Exhaled (approx.)
nitrogen78%78%
oxygen21%16%
carbon dioxide0.04%4%

Worked example: A class compares two limewater samples. Exhaled air turns the limewater cloudy faster because it contains more carbon dioxide. That is evidence of respiration, not proof that nitrogen vanished.

3 Respiration

Respiration Cells using oxygen to release energy from food. It is not the same as the chest moving. Chest movement that moves air in and out is ventilation (breathing). The two words are linked but not the same.

Worked sequence: food + oxygen → energy + carbon dioxide + water, inside cells. Ventilation brings the oxygen in and carries carbon dioxide out.

4 Pulse

Pulse A throb in an artery that is a clue to how often the heart is beating. Count beats in a known time, then scale to a minute if the rate stays steady. This lesson does not collect anyone’s private fitness diary.

Worked example: In a PE demo, a fictional pupil Jordan counts 18 beats in 15 s. \(18 \times 4 = 72\) beats per minute. The class records Jordan’s count, not a personal ranking of classmates.

5 Heart, blood and oxygen

Blood carries oxygen from the lungs to working tissues. The sequence on the schematic is:

  1. A heart pumps blood.
  2. B lungs add oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
  3. C body tissues use oxygen and produce carbon dioxide.

6 Sport and oxygen demand

In hard exercise, muscles need more oxygen. Breathing, blood and cell respiration work together: faster ventilation, a higher pulse, more oxygen delivered.

Worked sequence: A fictional sprinter Lee finishes a 200 m race. Pulse rises so blood reaches the muscles faster. Breathing stays fast after the sprint while extra carbon dioxide is cleared. That is demand, not a request for anyone’s private heart-rate file.

7 Pressure in water

Pressure How concentrated a force is on an area. Deeper water means more liquid above, so pressure on the body increases. That is a measurement idea, not a dare. Follow the teacher’s pool rules.

Worked example: A swimmer 2 m deeper than a partner has more water above them, so the pressure on the ears is higher. The lesson does not ask anyone to dive.

8 Buoyancy

Buoyancy The upward force (upthrust) from a liquid. If upthrust is bigger than weight, the object floats. Denser salty water can make floating easier. The online page does not replace a supervised pool session.

Worked example: A ball that sits low in a freshwater tank can sit higher in denser salty water because upthrust is greater. That is density, not a ranking of swimmers.

Quick reference

Gases

  • Air: mostly nitrogen + oxygen
  • Exhale: less O2, more CO2
  • Respiration: cells use O2

Sport

  • Pulse tracks heart rate
  • Heart pumps; lungs oxygenate
  • Depth → pressure; liquid → buoyancy
